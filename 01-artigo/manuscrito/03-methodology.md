## Methodology: Research Protocol, Data Collection, and Positionality

The study combines four components: a structured literature review; a qualitative, field-informed contextualization (Sections 3 and 4); a design-science component that proposes a technical model architecture and training strategy (Section 6); and a computational component that implements that strategy, generates a synthetic corpus, measures its properties before any detector is trained, and evaluates transfer to a public aerial benchmark against a ceiling trained on real data (Sections 7 to 10). These components are not run in parallel but built one on the other: the literature review identifies the gap the model is meant to address, the field contextualization supplies the empirical and situational grounding the model needs to be plausible, the design-science component translates that grounding into a concrete technical proposal, and the computational component executes it. The evaluation protocol of the fourth component was fixed before any model was trained; it is specified in Section 7.7 rather than here because it is long. That component instantiates the proposal of Section 6 on a different object class, the aerial detection of people, for the reason given at the opening of Section 7.

The study's acquisition parameters are not hypothetical. A police photogrammetric survey of the case study area was flown in August 2022, before and independently of this research, with a Zenmuse Z30 recording at 1920 by 1080, pointing straight down, 150 metres above ground. No image from that survey, and no product derived from it, is published in this article or in any material accompanying it. What the survey supplies is a sensor and an altitude: the virtual camera of Section 7.3 reproduces both, so that the acquisition conditions evaluated here are the ones a real flight over this territory actually used.

One of the researchers is an active-duty delegado (police chief) with operational familiarity with public-security activity in Jacarezinho. This gives the study access to a kind of data an outside researcher could not obtain on their own: direct professional experience, institutional knowledge of past operations, and a working understanding of the territory's built environment.

That same position, however, introduces a bias that has to be dealt with directly rather than mentioned in passing. Three problems in particular:

First, the researcher's institutional role shapes what gets noticed in the field. Observations tend to register tactical features (navigability, choke points, lines of sight) rather than the things a resident-centered or ethnographic account would foreground: daily life, informal economies, or the ways the community itself makes sense of its own space.

Second, access to the territory is itself a product of the researcher's institutional position, and that has consequences beyond who gets in. It affects what can be observed and, just as importantly, whether residents would be willing to speak at all, given the community's documented history of lethal police violence.

Third, none of this can be fully corrected for, only worked around. The study does so in three ways: by checking field observations against independent journalistic, human-rights, and academic sources on Jacarezinho (Sections 3 and 4); by marking tactically framed observations as such in the data record, rather than presenting them as neutral description; and by treating anything this research produces as a conceptual model, not a validated operational tool, because a data source drawn entirely from policing cannot, ethically or empirically, substitute for community consent or resident perspective.

Given these risks, the study operates under three constraints. No imagery identifying individual residents is collected or used. No precise, address-level building coordinates are published in this article or in any material derived from it. And any future field data collection would require an independent ethics review and a community engagement process external to the police institution, before any real-world deployment is considered.
