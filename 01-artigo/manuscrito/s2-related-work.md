## Related Work

### 2.1 Machine vision in informal settlements

Machine vision of informal settlements serves planning, humanitarian and development ends (da Silva et al., 2024). It maps settlements from satellite imagery (Gram-Hansen et al., 2019; Du et al., 2024), with street-view images (Hu et al., 2025) and from drone imagery of Slovo Park, Johannesburg (Sithole & Mhangara, 2025), with the building footprint as the object classified. Height enters as a feature or a reconstruction target: 2.5D and 3D features from UAV imagery improved classification of a dense, steeply sloped settlement in Kigali (Gevaert et al., 2016), and elevation models with learned height prediction reconstructed Nairobi, Kathmandu and Quito (Watson & Elliott, 2025). Neither treats the vertical structure of a settlement as the space in which an aerial system observes people, where acquisition conditions, rather than footprints, decide what can be seen.

### 2.2 Synthetic data and the sim-to-real gap in aerial detection

Domain randomisation varies rendered scenes widely enough that real images can be learned as one more variation (Tobin et al., 2017), and structured variants constrain placement with real spatial priors (Prakash et al., 2019); its reported gains have often been measured against ImageNet or random initialisation rather than against a detector already trained on real images of the target class (Tremblay et al., 2018). Against stronger starting points the sign is contested. On the full training split of VisDrone-DET (Zhu et al., 2022), the standard public benchmark for aerial detection in dense urban scenes, pretraining on two public synthetic corpora fell below training from scratch at most detector sizes, while a corpus designed for diverse human motion exceeded it and, combined with COCO (Common Objects in Context, a generic corpus of 80 classes), exceeded COCO alone (Yim et al., 2026). Mixing synthetic with real images fell below real-only training when real images were few (Shen et al., 2023a, 2023b), an instance of negative transfer, in which added source data lower accuracy on the target task.

Kiefer et al. (2022) trained three detectors purely on 40,000 game-engine images matched to VisDrone's traffic scenes and reported mean average precision, at an overlap threshold of 0.50, of 1.2, 2.4 and 10.2, against 24.6, 48.6 and 43.9 for the same architectures trained on the full real training split, every figure averaged over ten classes; as pretraining for a COCO-initialised YOLO, their corpus gained most with the smallest real set. Liao et al. (2026) built an aerial synthetic corpus of vehicles from photogrammetry: with generic assets in place of the reconstruction, YOLO11 scored 50.2 on the same measure against 74.5 (their Table 5), and removing variation in sun position, weather and viewing angle cost it 30.4 points averaged over their general and truncated subsets. Game engines also annotate aerial imagery automatically, of military vehicles rather than people in the game Arma 3 (Kim et al., 2026), and generate favela layouts for games and simulation rather than as training imagery (Berwaldt et al., 2020).

### 2.3 Scale, illumination and weapon detection from the air

A person seen from the air is a small object, and detectors lose instances smaller than the stride of their finest feature map; tiling at native scale has been adopted for this reason in surveillance footage of armed pedestrians (Hnoohom et al., 2022). Altitude acts through GSD: with a fixed sensor, halving the flying height doubles each linear dimension of the target and quadruples its area. Illumination acts through contrast against local clutter, which shadow both obscures in urban imagery (Dare, 2005; Du et al., 2018) and, as a person's own shadow under direct sun, can supply (Reilly et al., 2010).

Weapon detection has its own benchmarks, and none we found is aerial at the altitudes of this study (search scope in Online Appendix B5). Benchmarks that annotate a carried firearm are recorded from surveillance viewpoints (Salazar González et al., 2020; Lim et al., 2021; Ruiz-Santaquiteria et al., 2024; Z. Li et al., 2025); the public aerial datasets we found that label a person as armed were captured between 2 and 8 m above the ground and attach the label to an action class or a whole recording, not to a box (T. Li et al., 2021; Torkst et al., 2026); and the drone datasets we found with annotated firearms are restricted to law-enforcement agencies (Faheem et al., 2025) or unreleased (AlJundi et al., 2025). A detector may also learn a shortcut, a cue correlated with the label rather than the object itself (Geirhos et al., 2020): a weapon that is always the same asset, in the same pose, at the same contrast, lets it separate the classes on properties of the asset library. Setting weapons against objects handled in the same way has precedents in surveillance imagery (Pérez-Hernández et al., 2020) and in a rifle replica set against a similar umbrella from drones at 2 and 5 m (Torkst et al., 2026), and the corpora of this study adopt it (Section 4.2.2).

### 2.4 Capability claims, discretion and accountability in policing

Public administration research on algorithms in policing has two strands that bear on this article. The first concerns discretion: organisational context shaped how predictive policing systems were used in Berlin and Amsterdam (Meijer et al., 2021); street-level bureaucrats trusted artificial intelligence recommendations that confirmed their professional judgement (Selten et al., 2023); and status quo bias shaped how police officers perceived algorithmic discretion (Afzal & Panagiotopoulos, 2025). Transferring discretion to algorithms, which decide on statistical regularities and fail on anomalous cases, has been argued to threaten accountability and reason-giving (Anastasopoulos, 2026). An aerial alert that a person is armed would enter this space as one more input to decisions in which, on the premise stated in Section 3.3, whether a person was armed is a central category.

The second strand concerns accountability and legitimacy. Busuioc (2021) maps the challenges that algorithmic decision-making raises for public accountability, a relationship in which an actor informs a forum about its conduct, explains and justifies it, and may face consequences. In a survey experiment, use of artificial intelligence by a male-dominated police unit lowered female respondents' perceived fairness and trust, and use by a female-dominated unit did not (Gao & Riccucci, 2026). Across both strands the capability of the system is assumed: the research asks how officers, citizens and organisations respond to algorithmic outputs, not what a system was shown to perceive and under which conditions.

Two terms make that gap precise. A capability claim is an assertion, by a vendor, a developer or an agency, that a system can perform a perceptual task, here telling an armed person from an unarmed one in aerial imagery. An acquisition-conditioned capability statement is a capability claim written so that it can be checked: it names the acquisition conditions (GSD, camera pitch and illumination) at which the capability was or was not shown; the rate at which ordinary objects are called armed under them, with the confidence threshold and the prevalence of armed persons at which that rate was measured; the imagery on which it was measured; and the result that would withdraw the claim. It supplies the first element of account-giving in the sense of Busuioc (2021), information on what the system was shown to do and under which conditions, without which no explanation can be asked for and no sanction applied.

Existing instruments document part of this. Model cards report intended use, evaluation data, metrics and decision thresholds, with results disaggregated across factors that include the capturing instrument and the environment (Mitchell et al., 2019); internal audit attaches such documents to each stage of development (Raji et al., 2020); and the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (2024, Articles 11 and 13, Annexes III and IV), requires providers of high-risk systems, several law-enforcement uses among them, to document accuracy metrics, testing data and the circumstances that may affect accuracy, though its list of law-enforcement uses does not name aerial detection of armed persons. The statement adds four things: acquisition conditions treated as conditions of validity, outside which the claim does not hold, rather than as factors across which results are disaggregated; one mandated error metric, that rate, reported with its prevalence; a withdrawal condition fixed before testing; and attachment to procurement, as agency policy set ahead of any use of force, like the requirement to report pointing a firearm at a person that Jennings and Rubado (2017) associated, across police agencies in the United States, with fewer officer-involved gun deaths.
