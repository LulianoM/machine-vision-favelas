## Related Work

### 2.1 Machine vision in informal settlements and the vertical dimension

The UAV literature closest to this study is organised around trajectory planning and policing. Route planning takes three-dimensional complexity seriously: Rasoulie and Pashazadeh (2026) plan over terrain with peaks and no-fly zones, Xu (2025) through gridded urban space with high-rise obstacles, and Agrawal, Ratnoo, and Ghose (2017) along urban canyons by optical flow, where Jiang et al. (2026) document the degradation of global navigation satellite system (GNSS) positioning as a reason to fuse visual sensing. Aerial policing dissolves that complexity into planar formulations: Wang and Qian (2026) cast multi-target search as a travelling-salesman variant with no treatment of altitude or vertical obstacles, Chen, Liu, Hu, Zhang, and Pan (2025) optimise patrol over a street network, and Yan, Hsieh, and Lai (2025) route over patrol points to maximise coverage.

Machine vision applied to informal settlements is a separate niche, framed almost entirely around planning, humanitarian or development purposes. Gram-Hansen et al. (2019) combined domain knowledge, such as roofing materials, with machine learning classifiers to map settlements from satellite imagery where official records are poor. Du, Xing, Wang, Wei, and Zhang (2024) extended such pipelines with a texture-enhanced multiscale network, and Hu, Huang, Ren, and Zhang (2025) fused satellite imagery with street-view data to recover detail that overhead imagery alone cannot capture. Sithole and Mhangara (2025) classified the Slovo Park settlement in Johannesburg from drone imagery, at a resolution unattainable from satellite platforms, although the object classified remained the building footprint rather than its vertical extension. The same orientation runs through the digital-mapping and community-participation literature surveyed by da Silva, Kormann da Silva, and Westerholt (2024).

Most of this work treats settlements as two-dimensional footprints, a gap for Rio's favelas, where vertical growth (additional floors, rooftop units) bears on population density, structural risk and tactical navigability. Gevaert, Persello, Sliuzas, and Vosselman (2016) showed, in an unplanned settlement in Kigali, Rwanda, that dense buildings and steeply sloped terrain jointly challenge state-of-the-art classification, and that integrating 2D radiometric, 2.5D topographic and 3D geometric features from UAV imagery improves accuracy. Watson and Elliott (2025) used digital elevation models and deep-learning height prediction to reconstruct the vertical component of Nairobi, Kathmandu and Quito, noting that Nairobi's Mukuru settlement is omitted from one open building-footprint dataset and only partially mapped in OpenStreetMap. In the mapping literature the vertical dimension of informality thus enters as height and three-dimensional features for classifying and reconstructing the built fabric, and in the aerial policing literature it does not enter at all; the classification of what vertical growth adds (additional floors, rooftop units, changes of material) remains open, which Section 6.6 leaves as the morphological task for future work.

### 2.2 Synthetic data and the sim-to-real gap in aerial detection

Because labelled, consented, high-resolution imagery of informal settlements is scarce, this study draws on domain randomisation, developed by Tobin, Fong, Ray, Schneider, Zaremba, and Abbeel (2017) and extended by Prakash et al. (2019) to constrain the placement of synthetic elements with real-world spatial priors. Barisic, Petric, and Bogdan (2022) rendered a synthetic aerial dataset with randomised textures and reported gains in mean average precision (mAP) for detectors of UAVs on 2 real test sets. For industrial objects in a robotic setting, Horváth, Erdős, Istenes, Horváth, and Földi (2023) trained a YOLOv4 detector on domain-randomised images and reported a mAP at an intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.50 (mAP50) of 86.32 per cent in zero-shot and 97.38 per cent in one-shot transfer on 190 manually annotated real images. IoU is the overlap of a predicted and an annotated box divided by their union, and the threshold is the overlap at which a detection counts.

The benefit this body of work reports is conditional rather than general. Gains from synthetic pretraining have often been measured against ImageNet or random initialisation rather than against a detector already trained on real images of the target class (McCormac et al., 2017; Tremblay et al., 2018; Lima et al., 2025). Negative transfer (Wang et al., 2019) recurs in aerial person detection. On the full VisDrone training split, Yim et al. (2026) found pretraining on either of 2 public synthetic corpora scoring below training from scratch at most detector sizes, while a corpus designed for diverse human motion scored above it and, with COCO (Common Objects in Context, a generic object-detection corpus of 80 classes), above COCO alone. Mixing synthetic with real images fell below the real-only baseline in low-shot VisDrone training (Shen et al., 2023a) and harmed the larger COCO-pretrained detectors most when real images were fewest, much less once the shares were balanced (Shen et al., 2023b). With unbalanced batches, mixing biased a drone detector of people at sea towards the more numerous synthetic images (Martinez-Esteso et al., 2025). The mechanisms offered come from training on renders alone: retraining a feature extractor pretrained on real images degraded detection, whereas freezing it came close to training on real images (Hinterstoisser et al., 2019), although freezing hurt with highly randomised scenes (Tremblay et al., 2018). The starting weights matter most when real data is scarce (He et al., 2019), and the image domain is the factor that most determines positive transfer (Mensink et al., 2022).

The domain-randomisation results above concern objects that fill a substantial share of the frame; a person seen from a survey altitude does not, and the transfer evidence closest to that task comes from VisDrone. VisDrone (Zhu et al., 2022) is the standard public benchmark for aerial detection in dense urban scenes, recorded from uncontrolled acquisition; its detection track, VisDrone-DET, is the one used here, and Section 4.3 describes the subsets. Kiefer, Ott, and Zell (2022) trained 3 detectors purely on 40,000 game-engine images generated to match VisDrone's traffic scenes and reported mAP50 of 1.2, 2.4 and 10.2 against 24.6, 48.6 and 43.9 for the same architectures trained on the full real training split, every figure averaged over 10 classes. As pretraining for a COCO-initialised YOLO, the same corpus gave its largest gain on VisDrone with the smallest real set, and 10,000 of its images scored below no synthetic pretraining at every real-data size on their maritime benchmark. Liao et al. (2026) identify where the synthetic fraction is won or lost. Their aerial synthetic corpus is built from photogrammetry, with vehicles as the object class and evaluation on the reconstructed site, and they ablate 2 factors. Replacing the photogrammetric reconstruction with generic assets costs 27.0 mAP50 points on YOLO11 and 22 to 23 on average across the architectures they test; removing variation in sun position, weather and viewing angle costs 30.4 on the same detector and 17.5 on average.

Game engines have also served as generators of annotated aerial data in their own right. Kim et al. (2026) use the tactical simulation game Arma 3 to render synchronised visible and thermal (RGB-T) images from several aerial viewpoints, with boxes annotated automatically from the engine's geometric metadata; their benchmark covers 12 categories of military vehicles and equipment rather than people, and pretraining on it raised mAP50 over ImageNet initialisation on one visible and one thermal real aerial benchmark. This study uses a headless procedural generator rather than a game engine, because a game can be driven from outside only by reading the screen and clicking in the foreground, whereas the generator runs unattended with a deterministic seed. Procedural generation has also been applied to the informal settlement itself: Berwaldt, Bettker, and Pozzer (2020) generate favela road networks, lots and alleys on arbitrary terrain, routing roads across steep slopes, as layouts for games and simulation rather than as training imagery for detection.

### 2.3 Small-object aerial detection, GSD, and person and weapon detection

A person seen from the air is a small object, and the scale at which it reaches the network governs whether it can be detected. The detector family of Section 4.5 computes its finest feature map at a stride of 8 pixels, so an instance whose shorter side falls below 8 pixels occupies less than one cell of that map and is close to invisible by construction. Tiling at native scale has been adopted for this reason in surveillance footage of armed pedestrians, where downsampling erases small weapons (Hnoohom et al., 2022). Altitude acts on the target through GSD: with a fixed sensor, halving the flying height doubles each linear dimension of the target and quadruples its area, so acquisition altitude is a domain shift in its own right.

Illumination enters through contrast against local clutter. Dare (2005) reports that in dense urban scenes shadow cast by buildings obscures much of the information in high-resolution imagery and can corrupt classification. Du et al. (2018) found the vehicle detectors of their drone benchmark worse in daylight than at night, which they attribute to more objects and a more cluttered background with shadow interference, while its trackers did better in daylight. Under a direct sun a person also casts a shadow of their own, which Reilly et al. (2010) used as a cue for detecting people from the air. Aerial survey specifications nonetheless require imagery free of cloud and cloud shadow and a minimum elevation of the sun (National Geodetic Survey, 2002; Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors [RICS], 2021), the practice Section 6.3 revisits.

Weapon detection has its own benchmarks, and none we found is aerial at the altitudes of this study. Those that annotate a carried firearm are recorded from surveillance viewpoints (Salazar González et al., 2020; Lim et al., 2021; Ruiz-Santaquiteria et al., 2024; Z. Li et al., 2025); the public aerial datasets we found that label a person as armed were captured between 2 and 8 m above the ground and attach the label to an action class or a whole recording rather than to a box (T. Li et al., 2021; Torkst et al., 2026); and the drone datasets we found with annotated firearms are restricted to law-enforcement agencies (Faheem et al., 2025) or unreleased (AlJundi et al., 2025). A second strand concerns corpus design: a weapon that is always the same asset, in the same pose, at the same contrast, lets a detector separate the classes on properties of the asset library rather than on anything visible in the world. Setting weapons against objects handled in the same way has precedents: in surveillance imagery, Pérez-Hernández et al. (2020) set a pistol and a knife against a smartphone, a banknote, a purse and a card, and from drones hovering at 2 and 5 m, Torkst et al. (2026) set a rifle replica against a visually similar umbrella. Section 4.2.2 describes how the corpora of this study adopt that design.

### 2.4 What the literature establishes and what remains open

The literature reviewed here establishes four things. Geometry origin and rendering variation both matter: a generic base in place of a photogrammetric one, or the loss of variation in sun, weather and viewing angle, costs tens of mAP50 points in the ablations of Liao et al. (2026). Purely synthetic training recovers a fraction of what real data achieves in dense urban scenes (Kiefer, Ott, and Zell, 2022). A modest volume of real data improves generalisation from synthetic-only training in favourable cases, although the same literature records synthetic corpora lowering accuracy below real-only training. And altitude acts through GSD, so aerial datasets that label an armed person within a few metres of the ground say nothing about survey altitudes.

Three things remain open. The first is the GSD at which a carried firearm is separable from an ordinary elongated object seen from the air, for which the precedents stand at ground level or a few metres above it. The second is synthetic pretraining for person detection at nadir over a dense informal settlement, since the transfer results above come from traffic, maritime and controlled scenes, none of them a favela. The third is an aerial test set for the carried firearm at survey altitudes, which the search of Section 2.3 did not find. The research questions of Section 1.3 are posed against the first two openings, and the third is why RQ3 is answered on synthetic imagery alone.
