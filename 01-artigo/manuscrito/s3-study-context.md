## Study Context

This section is background, not data collected for the study: every figure is that of the public source cited, most of them not peer-reviewed because the events are recent, and no fieldwork was done (Section 4.1).

### 3.1 Lethal force, its oversight and aerial systems in Rio de Janeiro

Killing by the police in the state of Rio de Janeiro rose in 2025 while the homicide rate held at 17.1 per 100,000 (Online Appendix A1): deaths from police intervention rose from 703 in 2024 to 798 in 2025, 20.5 per cent of all intentional violent deaths in the state and one of the highest such shares in Brazil, and the number of police officers killed rose from 28 to 51 while it fell nationally (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, Tables 6 and 8; Sousa, 2026).

The main external check on that lethality has been judicial. In 2019 the Brazilian Socialist Party and civil society organisations filed Arguição de Descumprimento de Preceito Fundamental (ADPF) 635 before the Supreme Federal Court (STF) against police lethality in favelas, and a June 2020 injunction restricted incursions to absolutely exceptional circumstances (Agência Brasil, 2025). The restrictions did not prevent Operação Exceptis, conducted by the Civil Police in Jacarezinho on 6 May 2021, which left 27 civilians and 1 officer dead, at the time the city's deadliest police operation (Human Rights Watch, 2021). On 3 April 2025 the STF concluded the case, partially validating a state plan to reduce lethality and reversing several of the restrictions (STF, 2025a, 2025b).

On 28 October 2025 about 2,500 civil and military police officers launched Operação Contenção against the Comando Vermelho in the Complexo do Alemão and the Complexo da Penha. With 121 dead by the official count and 132 by the Public Defender's Office, it was the deadliest police operation on record in Brazil (Fichino, 2025; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025; Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026), and it now anchors the debate over whether ADPF 635 constrains lethal police conduct (Agência Pública, 2025).

Aerial systems already belong to this setting, on both sides: Comando Vermelho fighters have dropped improvised explosives on police units from consumer drones, and the Civil Police has since expanded its Coordenadoria de Operações com Aeronaves Não Tripuladas, which flies UAVs for intelligence and real-time operational monitoring (CNN, 2026; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025).

### 3.2 Jacarezinho as the motivating case

Jacarezinho, in the city's North Zone, covers roughly 40 hectares and is considered the third-largest favela after Rocinha and the Complexo do Alemão (RioOnWatch, 2019); its population is uncertain (Online Appendix A1). Its built form is what makes it the motivating case: overlapping, vertically stacked construction; narrow alleys; rooftop slabs (lajes) that are occupied or extended; and occlusion between structures. That such construction also blocks lines of sight from the ground is a premise of this study, not a finding of the sources cited here. The procedural settlements of Section 4.2 target this general morphology, not the geometry of Jacarezinho, and the site is not a test set.

### 3.3 Why the armed and unarmed judgement is the ethical core

We take as a premise, not as a finding of the sources cited here, that whether the person engaged was armed is a central category in decisions to use lethal force. It is a discretionary judgement made at speed by front-line officers, in narrow alleys and on rooftops, among residents who are present throughout: street-level discretion of the kind public administration research has examined in Brazilian police officers' use of violence (Alcadipani et al., 2024) and, increasingly, under algorithmic support (Afzal & Panagiotopoulos, 2025).

The two errors of an aerial system assisting that judgement are not symmetric. Missing an armed person withholds a warning the officer did not have before. Marking an unarmed resident as armed, because the resident carries a broom, a length of pipe or a rolled mat, gives a person who does not meet it the category that justifies lethal force. Street-level officers have also been found to trust an algorithmic recommendation most when it confirms what they already believe (Selten et al., 2023), as an armed alert would during an incursion against an armed group. The rate at which a detector calls an ordinary carried object armed is therefore the quantity that matters ethically; Section 5.4 measures it, on synthetic imagery only, and Section 6.4 interprets it.
