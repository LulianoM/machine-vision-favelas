## Study Context

### 3.1 Policing, lethality and territorial control in Rio de Janeiro

What this subsection and the next can and cannot support is stated at the end of Section 3.2.

Homicide in Rio de Janeiro has fallen since the mid-1990s. The state series of the Instituto de Segurança Pública do Rio de Janeiro (ISP-RJ), which begins in 1991, peaked in 1994 at 63.13 intentional homicides per 100,000 inhabitants (ISP-RJ, n.d.-a). The Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública puts the rate at 17.1 in 2024, 10.8 per cent below 2023 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2025, its Table 3), and at 17.1 again in 2025 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, its Table 3). In the city, intentional homicides by police district fell from 2,574 in 2003, the first year of that series, to 965 in 2025, a figure that from 2024 excludes femicides (ISP-RJ, n.d.-b).

Lethal police intervention did not follow in 2025. The 20th Anuário Brasileiro de Segurança Pública records 798 deaths from police intervention in the state in 2025, up from 703 in 2024 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, its Table 8), 20.5 per cent of all intentional violent deaths and one of the highest such ratios in Brazil (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026; Sousa, 2026). Police officers killed in the state rose from 28 to 51, an increase of 82.1 per cent, against a national fall from 144 to 139: the state accounted for 51 of the 139 police deaths in the country (Barros, 2026; Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, its Table 6).

On 6 May 2021 the Civil Police carried out Operação Exceptis in Jacarezinho with roughly 200 to 250 officers, armoured vehicles, helicopters and a sniper. It killed 27 civilians and 1 officer, at the time the city's deadliest police operation (Human Rights Watch, 2021; Sullivan et al., 2021), and human-rights organisations asked whether some of the dead had been executed (Al Jazeera, 2021; Human Rights Watch, 2021).

In 2019 the Brazilian Socialist Party and civil society organisations filed Arguição de Descumprimento de Preceito Fundamental (ADPF) 635 before the Supreme Federal Court (STF) against police lethality in favelas; a June 2020 injunction restricted incursions to absolutely exceptional circumstances (Agência Brasil, 2025; Movisafe América Latina, 2025), and the case ran for more than 5 years. On 3 April 2025 the STF concluded it, partially validating a state plan to reduce lethality, reversing several restrictions in force since 2020 and requiring a plan to reoccupy areas held by criminal organisations (Gazeta do Povo, 2025; STF, 2025a, 2025b). Lethality had already fallen by more than 60 per cent under the earlier restrictions, from 1,814 deaths in 2019 to 699 in 2024 (JOTA, 2025), a figure that differs from the 703 of the Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública (2026) for reasons neither source documents; critics found the ruling either too weak or too intrusive (Causa Operária, 2025; Gazeta do Povo, 2025).

Less than 7 months later, on 28 October 2025, civil and military police launched Operação Contenção against the Comando Vermelho in the Complexo do Alemão and the Complexo da Penha, 26 communities, with about 2,500 officers, 32 armoured vehicles, 12 demolition vehicles and 2 helicopters (Couto, 2025; Fichino, 2025). Its judicial basis, by the state's report to the STF, was 51 arrest warrants of the 42nd Criminal Court of the capital, within a total of 100 the state sought to serve (Fichino, 2025). The official count of 30 October 2025 was 121 dead, 117 civilians and 4 officers (Couto & Figueiredo, 2025; Fichino, 2025), rising to 122 when a fifth officer died in November (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026; Reuters, 2025). On 29 October the state's Public Defender's Office had counted 132, 128 civilians and 4 officers (Falcão & Couto, 2025; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025). By either count it was the deadliest police operation on record in Brazil (Fichino, 2025; Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026); the President called it a massacre (CBS News, 2025), the United Nations expressed grave concern (OPEB, 2025), and the operation now anchors the debate over whether ADPF 635 constrains lethal police conduct (Agência Pública, 2025). Comando Vermelho fighters also dropped improvised explosives on police units from consumer drones (CNN, 2026; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025), and the Civil Police has since expanded its Coordenadoria de Operações com Aeronaves Não Tripuladas, which flies UAVs for intelligence and real-time operational monitoring (CNN, 2026).

Behind these episodes, criminal organisations hold durable, often decades-long control over particular favelas, financed by drug trafficking (Lessing, 2008; Misse, 2011) and contested by rival factions and by milícias, paramilitary groups often formed by security personnel (InSight Crime, 2026; Misse, 2011). The Comando Vermelho, the principal one, emerged as a prison gang on Ilha Grande (Lessing, 2008), was first named in a prison director's report in 1979 (Misse, 2011) and in 2020 was estimated at around 30,000 members (InSight Crime, 2026). Narrow streets force special units to leave their armoured vehicles and plan routes through maze-like favelas (Grillo, 2014); incursions have met barricades, concrete at the entrance to Jacarezinho in 2021 and burning cars on access roads in 2025 (Sullivan et al., 2021; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025). Two further obstacles are the premise of this study, not findings of those sources: vertically stacked construction that blocks lines of sight, and the absence of reliable, up-to-date maps of informal circulation routes, which leaves planning dependent on outdated or incomplete intelligence.

### 3.2 Jacarezinho as a morphological reference case

Jacarezinho, in the North Zone of the city, is among its largest favelas, with an uncertain population: municipal figures put it at approximately 37,000 residents (RioOnWatch, 2019), the 2022 national census recorded 29,766 in the favela (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística, 2024, its Table 5) and 35,458 in the administrative subdistrict of that name (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística, n.d.), and community and journalistic sources commonly cite more than 60,000 (Caminhos Languages, 2025; Mistler-Ferguson, 2022), a discrepancy this article cannot resolve. It covers roughly 40 hectares, is considered the third-largest after Rocinha and the Complexo do Alemão, was already the largest by 1960 with roughly 23,000 residents, and has the highest concentration of Afro-Brazilian residents among the city's favelas (RioOnWatch, 2019). The Comando Vermelho has long dominated it (Mistler-Ferguson, 2022); in May 2021 fighting took place in narrow alleys and on rooftops, with concrete barricades at the entrance (Sullivan et al., 2021), and on 19 January 2022 more than 1,200 military police officers occupied it (Mistler-Ferguson, 2022).

Its morphology makes it the reference case: overlapping, vertically stacked construction; narrow alleyways; rooftop slabs (lajes) that are occupied or extended; buildings with no standardised geometry; access routes absent from official maps; and visual occlusion between structures. The synthetic settlements of Section 4.2 approximate this general morphology (abutting dwellings, narrow alleys, upper storeys offset from the floor below) but not the geometry of Jacarezinho: they are procedural, and no imagery or geometry of the territory enters them or any measurement reported here.

Sections 3.1 and 3.2 draw on public sources only (Section 4.1). They are background that motivates the study, not data collected or analysed for it: no field protocol, interview, survey, observation record or resident testimony exists, and every number in them comes from the sources cited. The sources are largely journalistic and institutional rather than peer-reviewed, because the events are recent.

### 3.3 The technological turn in public security

The armed and unarmed judgement (Section 3.5, below) reaches an aerial system at the end of a movement in public security from discretion reported after the fact to discretion mediated by instruments, which the public administration literature has followed step by step. It starts with the street-level officer, whose use of force is a discretionary act that reporting rules document afterwards and that, for Brazilian military police at protests, has been traced to the conditions of their work rather than to individual disposition (Alcadipani, Lotta, & Cohen, 2024). Body-worn cameras brought recording into the act itself: they were studied as an intervention in the discretion of the officer deciding on an arrest (Headley, Baker, & Kang, 2025), and their footage enters the evaluation of a use of force alongside the identity of those seen in it (Wright, Gaozhao, & Houston, 2024). Predictive policing moved the instrument upstream, to the allocation of the patrol, and there the instrument did not fix its own effect: systems of the same kind were put to different uses by the Berlin and Amsterdam police, each shaped by the organisational practice around it rather than by the system alone (Meijer, Lorenz, & Wessels, 2021). Nor does it fix the officer's response: in a vignette experiment, the algorithmic recommendation that contradicted the street-level bureaucrat's own reading of the case was the one set aside, so what an instrument reports reaches the decision filtered through a judgement already formed (Selten, Robeer, & Grimmelikhuijsen, 2023). Whether the public grants legitimacy to a police force that uses artificial intelligence is an empirical question (Gao & Riccucci, 2026), and a twenty-year retrospective of one security and justice organisation shows digital transformation as a long alternation of central steering and freedom for units (Selten, van Loo, & Klievink, 2026). With UAVs the instrument took to the air, and the literature met it with questions of surveillance, what the domestic use of drones does to privacy, accountability and the relation between agency and watched (West & Bowman, 2016) and what constitutes a surveillance at all (Frazier, 2016).

Rio de Janeiro has taken the same steps in compressed form: the Civil Police unit that flies UAVs over operations, and the consumer drones the armed groups fly against it, are those of Section 3.1.

Every step in this literature takes the perception beneath it as given: the instrument is studied for what it does to discretion, legitimacy and organisation, and whether it sees what it is credited with seeing is not asked (Section 1.2). Over the terrain of Section 3.2 that premise is the hard part: stacked construction, narrow alleys and occlusion decide what reaches a camera before any discretion or trust comes into play, and whether a person is armed has to be shown visible at the altitude and resolution flown, which Sections 4 and 5 take up.

Which authority would authorise an aerial detector over an operation, and under what review, this article leaves open: ADPF 635 shows the STF setting the conditions under which police incursions proceed (Section 3.1), but nothing cited here says whether the plan it validated in 2025 reaches the instruments an operation flies. The condition this article offers to whichever authority decides is that of Section 6.4.

### 3.4 The ethical basis for synthetic training

Real imagery of the people this study concerns cannot be collected ethically; this subsection states why, and what synthetic training resolves and leaves standing.

The policing of Section 3.1 is among the most lethal in Brazil: 798 deaths from police intervention across the state in 2025, the aggregate for the year rather than the toll of any one operation (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026), 27 civilians killed in one operation in Jacarezinho in 2021 (Human Rights Watch, 2021; Sullivan et al., 2021) and 117 or more in one day in 2025 (Couto & Figueiredo, 2025; Fichino, 2025). A resident asked to consent to aerial photography, by or for the institution that conducts those operations, in a territory under the armed control of a faction (Section 3.2), can neither refuse freely nor foresee the use of the image; ADPF 635, in which the STF restricted incursions from June 2020 and which it concluded in April 2025 (STF, 2025b; Agência Pública, 2025), records how contested the presence of the police there is. A study aligned with a policing application therefore cannot obtain community consent; any field imagery would require independent ethics review and community engagement external to the police institution, which has not taken place (Sections 4.1 and 6.6).

Two alternatives were rejected. Collecting imagery during operations would image residents under the conditions that void consent, and would be surveillance in the sense the public administration literature gives the domestic use of drones (West & Bowman, 2016); using imagery a police institution already holds from its own flights (CNN, 2026) would inherit the same absence of consent and tie the corpus to an address-level record of a specific territory.

What synthesis resolves is specific: no resident is imaged, no geometry or coordinate of the territory enters the generator (Section 4.2), and every frame is reproducible from a seed, so a corpus can be regenerated and destroyed without anyone having been recorded; no other procedure we identified yields a labelled corpus of the required morphology under that condition (Section 4.1).

What it does not resolve is as specific. Not dual use: a detector trained without imaging anyone can be pointed at residents once deployed, and the training data do not change what the system is for (Section 1.1). Not the gap to reality: it trades the consent problem for the transfer problem, since what is learned on a procedural settlement must still hold on real aerial imagery, where transfer is limited in dense urban scenes (Kiefer, Ott, & Zell, 2022), which Section 5 measures. And not the harm the study measures: the false armed call on a resident carrying an ordinary object, whose asymmetry with the missed weapon Section 3.5 (below) sets out and Section 6.4 reads as dual use.

Two constraints, fixed before any corpus was generated and stated in full in Section 4.1, follow: the territory itself stays out of the generator, the corpora and every measurement, and nothing produced here is treated as a validated operational tool.

### 3.5 Why the armed and unarmed judgement is the ethical core

We take as a premise, not as a finding of the sources cited here, that whether the person engaged was armed is a central category in decisions to use lethal force. It is a visual judgement made at speed, in narrow alleys and on rooftops, among residents who are present throughout, and it is increasingly proposed as one an aerial system could assist; such systems already fly over policed territory in the state (Section 3.1).

This article treats that judgement as a measurable acquisition question rather than an assumed capability (Section 1.1): the acquisition fixes the GSD and with it the pixels a person and a carried object occupy (Section 4.2.4).

The two errors are not symmetric. Missing an armed person withholds a warning from the officers. Marking an unarmed resident as armed, because the resident carries a broom, a length of pipe or a rolled mat, supplies the category that justifies lethal force to a person who does not meet it. The rate at which a detector calls an ordinary carried object a weapon is therefore the quantity that matters ethically: Section 5.4 measures it, on synthetic imagery only, and Section 6.4 reads it as the concrete form of the dual-use harm.
