## Study Context

### 3.1 Policing, lethality and territorial control in Rio de Janeiro

What this subsection and the next can and cannot support is stated at the end of Section 3.2.

Homicide in Rio de Janeiro has fallen since the mid-1990s. The state series of the Instituto de Segurança Pública do Rio de Janeiro (ISP-RJ), which begins in 1991, peaked in 1994 at 63.13 intentional homicides per 100,000 inhabitants (ISP-RJ, n.d.-a). The Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública puts the rate at 17.1 in 2024, 10.8 per cent below 2023 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2025, Table 3), and at 17.1 again in 2025 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, Table 3). In the city, intentional homicides by police district fell from 2,574 in 2003, the first year of that series, to 965 in 2025, a figure that from 2024 excludes femicides (ISP-RJ, n.d.-b).

Lethal police intervention has not followed. The 20th Anuário Brasileiro de Segurança Pública records 798 deaths from police intervention in the state in 2025, up from 703 in 2024 (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, Table 8), 20.5 per cent of all intentional violent deaths and one of the highest such ratios in Brazil (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026; Sousa, 2026). Police officers killed in the state rose from 28 to 51, an increase of 82.1 per cent, against a national fall from 144 to 139: the state accounted for 51 of the 139 police deaths in the country (Barros, 2026; Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026, Table 6).

On 6 May 2021 the civil police carried out Operação Exceptis in Jacarezinho with roughly 200 to 250 officers, armoured vehicles, helicopters and a sniper. It killed 27 civilians and 1 officer, at the time the city's deadliest police operation (Human Rights Watch, 2021; Sullivan et al., 2021), and human-rights organisations asked whether some of the dead had been executed (Al Jazeera, 2021; Human Rights Watch, 2021).

In 2019 the Brazilian Socialist Party and civil society organisations filed Arguição de Descumprimento de Preceito Fundamental (ADPF) 635 before the Supreme Federal Court (STF) against police lethality in favelas; a June 2020 injunction restricted incursions to absolutely exceptional circumstances (Agência Brasil, 2025; Movisafe América Latina, 2025), and the case ran for nearly 6 years. On 3 April 2025 the STF concluded it, partially validating a state plan to reduce lethality, reversing several restrictions in force since 2020 and requiring a plan to reoccupy areas held by criminal organisations (Gazeta do Povo, 2025; STF, 2025a, 2025b). Lethality had already fallen by more than 60 per cent under the earlier restrictions, from 1,814 deaths in 2019 to 699 in 2024 (JOTA, 2025), which differs from the 703 of the Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública (2026) for undocumented reasons; critics found the ruling either too weak or too intrusive (Causa Operária, 2025; Gazeta do Povo, 2025).

Less than 7 months later, on 28 October 2025, civil and military police launched Operação Contenção against the Comando Vermelho in the Complexo do Alemão and the Complexo da Penha, 26 communities, with about 2,500 officers, 32 armoured vehicles, 12 demolition vehicles and 2 helicopters (Couto, 2025; Fichino, 2025). Its judicial basis, by the state's report to the STF, was 51 arrest warrants of the 42nd Criminal Court of the capital, within a total of 100 the state sought to serve (Fichino, 2025). The official count of 30 October 2025 was 121 dead, 117 civilians and 4 officers (Couto & Figueiredo, 2025; Fichino, 2025), rising to 122 when a fifth officer died in November (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026; Reuters, 2025). On 29 October the state's Public Defender's Office had counted 132, 128 civilians and 4 officers (Falcão & Couto, 2025; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025). By either count it was the deadliest police operation on record in Brazil (Fichino, 2025; Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026); the President called it a massacre (CBS News, 2025), the United Nations expressed grave concern (OPEB, 2025), and the operation now anchors the debate over whether ADPF 635 constrains lethal police conduct (Agência Pública, 2025). Comando Vermelho fighters also dropped improvised explosives on police units from consumer drones (CNN, 2026; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025), and the Civil Police has since expanded its Coordenadoria de Operações com Aeronaves Não Tripuladas, which flies UAVs for intelligence and real-time operational monitoring (CNN, 2026).

Behind these episodes, criminal organisations hold durable, often decades-long control over particular favelas, financed by drug trafficking and contested by rival factions and by milícias (paramilitary groups often formed by security personnel). The Comando Vermelho, the principal one, emerged as a prison gang on Ilha Grande (Lessing, 2008), was first named in a prison director's report in 1979 (Misse, 2011) and in 2020 was estimated at around 30,000 members (InSight Crime, 2026). Narrow streets force special units to leave their armoured vehicles and plan routes through maze-like favelas (Grillo, 2014); incursions have met barricades, concrete at the entrance to Jacarezinho in 2021 and burning cars on access roads in 2025 (Sullivan et al., 2021; Sullivan & da Cruz, 2025). Two further obstacles are the premise of this study, not findings of those sources: vertically stacked construction that blocks lines of sight, and the absence of reliable, up-to-date maps of informal circulation routes, which leaves planning dependent on outdated or incomplete intelligence.

### 3.2 Jacarezinho as a morphological reference case

Jacarezinho, in the North Zone of the city, is among its largest favelas, with an uncertain population: municipal figures put it at approximately 37,000 residents (RioOnWatch, 2019), the 2022 national census recorded 35,458, and community and journalistic sources commonly cite more than 60,000 (Caminhos Languages, 2025; Mistler-Ferguson, 2022), a discrepancy this article cannot resolve. It covers roughly 40 hectares, is considered the third-largest after Rocinha and the Complexo do Alemão, was already the largest by 1960 with roughly 23,000 residents, and has the highest concentration of Afro-Brazilian residents among the city's favelas (RioOnWatch, 2019). The Comando Vermelho has long dominated it (Mistler-Ferguson, 2022); in May 2021 fighting took place in narrow alleys and on rooftops, with concrete barricades at the entrance (Sullivan et al., 2021), and on 19 January 2022 more than 1,200 military police officers occupied it (Mistler-Ferguson, 2022).

Its morphology makes it the reference case: steep slopes; overlapping, vertically stacked construction; narrow alleyways; rooftop slabs (lajes) that are occupied or extended; buildings with no standardised geometry; access routes absent from official maps; and visual occlusion between structures. The synthetic settlements of Section 4.2 approximate this general morphology (abutting dwellings, narrow alleys, upper storeys offset from the floor below) but not the geometry of Jacarezinho: they are procedural, and no imagery or geometry of the territory enters them or any measurement reported here.

Sections 3.1 and 3.2 draw on public sources and on the professional familiarity of one author with the territory (Section 4.1). They are background that motivates the study, not data collected or analysed for it: no field protocol, interview, survey, observation record or resident testimony exists, and no number in this article derives from that familiarity. The sources are largely journalistic and institutional rather than peer-reviewed, because the events are recent.

### 3.3 Why the armed and unarmed judgement is the ethical core

The legal and operational category on which the use of lethal force turns is whether the person engaged was armed. That is a visual judgement made at speed, in narrow alleys and on rooftops, among residents who are present throughout, and it is increasingly proposed as one an aerial system could assist; such systems already fly over policed territory in the state (Section 3.1).

This article treats that judgement as a measurable acquisition question rather than an assumed capability (Section 1.1): the acquisition fixes the GSD and with it the pixels a person and a carried object occupy (Section 4.2.4).

The two errors are not symmetric. Missing an armed person fails the operation. Marking an unarmed resident as armed, because the resident carries a broom, a length of pipe or a rolled mat, supplies the category that justifies lethal force to a person who does not meet it. The rate at which a detector calls an ordinary carried object a weapon is therefore the quantity that matters ethically: Section 5.4 measures it, on synthetic imagery only, and Section 6.4 reads it as the concrete form of the dual-use harm.
