## Conclusion

Trained on synthetic imagery of a dense settlement alone, a detector recovers a small fraction of the person-detection accuracy that real training images give on a public aerial benchmark. It recovers 1.6 per cent of the ceiling for the reference acquisition, nadir at 150 m, 9.7 per cent with the same settlement rendered from an oblique camera, and 15.3 per cent, averaged over three training runs, for the best oblique configuration. Of the parameters RQ1 names, camera pitch determines most of that fraction, through the apparent shape of the target rather than its size, which was matched; settlement variety moves it less, and the factor of 6.0 between the two geometries rests on one training run per model. On synthetic frames, detection was weakest under hard sun and, on mAP50, strongest under overcast, level with low sun, matching the two extremes of contrast over clutter, a result that stays conditional until it is tested on real imagery.

Once 324 or 647 real training images are available, the synthetic corpus does not add accuracy, whether used as pretraining or mixed into the training set. A generic initialisation given the same real images finished above the synthetic start by 0.0716 of mAP50 on the seed mean at 5 per cent and by 0.0614 at 10 per cent, and above the joint models by 0.0637 and 0.0281, differences that pass the interval reading, the first also the seed reading, none the 30 per cent reading. What narrows as real data grows is a cost, measured under one fine-tuning schedule that was not searched.

On synthetic imagery alone, a detector separates a figure carrying a firearm from one carrying an everyday elongated object only partially at 1.14 and 2.44 cm per pixel, and at 4.88 the absence remains argued rather than measured, because the one model trained there failed its validity condition. At those distances it calls between one object in six and one in four armed on the selection splits, and 25 against 12.5 per cent on new frames for the 35 m corpus and its twin at 75 m, and the finer distance did not reduce that rate. The cue may be the length of what is carried rather than the form of a firearm, no similarity device without a detector separates the classes, and the distinction has not been tested on real imagery.

A claim that a system can tell an armed person from an unarmed one from the air is, at the altitudes and sensors described here, a claim that can be checked before it is believed, and this article declines to make it. Showing where a capability is absent, and why, is a contribution of the same kind as demonstrating it, and in this domain the more useful of the two. What remains open is not primarily technical: a system that can tell an armed person from an unarmed one and a system that is safe to point at Jacarezinho are different objects, and nothing in the engineering reported here decides the second. That decision belongs to a process that includes the residents of the territory, and it has not taken place.

## Declarations
