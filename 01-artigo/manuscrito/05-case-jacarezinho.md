## Rio de Janeiro's Favelas and the Case of Jacarezinho

Favelas are a defining feature of Rio de Janeiro's urban landscape, housing a substantial share of the city's population in self-built, incrementally constructed settlements that have historically developed outside formal planning and land-titling processes. Among the largest is Jacarezinho, located in the city's North Zone, bordering the neighborhoods of Jacaré, Méier, Engenho Novo, and Triagem. Estimates of its population vary considerably depending on source and methodology: municipal figures place it at approximately 37,000 residents (RioOnWatch, 2019), the 2022 national census recorded 35,458, while community and journalistic sources commonly cite figures above 60,000 (Ad Infinitum, 2023; Caminhos Languages, 2025), a discrepancy that is itself illustrative of the broader difficulty official spatial and demographic infrastructures have in capturing dense informal settlements accurately. Covering an area of roughly 40 hectares, Jacarezinho is considered the third-largest favela in Rio de Janeiro after Rocinha and Complexo do Alemão.

Jacarezinho's growth traces back to the early twentieth century, accelerating during the industrial boom of the nearby Méier district after World War II; by 1960 it had already become the largest favela in the city, with a population of roughly 23,000. It is also documented as having the highest concentration of Afro-Brazilian residents among Rio's favelas, a legacy connected to its historical role as a settlement point for formerly enslaved people and their descendants during the city's industrialization (RioOnWatch, 2019). Like many of Rio's favelas, it has for decades been under the territorial control of the Comando Vermelho, which uses the community's dense, irregular built environment (narrow alleyways, elevated walkways, and a layout that has grown organically rather than through planned infrastructure) both to conduct trafficking operations and to limit the operational reach of security forces (Ad Infinitum, 2023).

This dynamic culminated in the May 2021 Operação Exceptis, discussed above, and again in a subsequent large-scale intervention in January 2023, when more than 1,200 military police officers occupied the community as part of the state's "Cidade Integrada" (Integrated City) program, an initiative pairing territorial security operations with promised social and infrastructure investment (Ad Infinitum, 2023). Whether such interventions durably alter the community's underlying informality, its unmapped circulation routes, its vertically improvised construction, and the persistence of factional control even after major operations, remains contested. What this study engages is narrower, and prior to it: whether an aerial system flown over this morphology can resolve the distinction on which the use of lethal force is justified, and under what acquisition conditions.
