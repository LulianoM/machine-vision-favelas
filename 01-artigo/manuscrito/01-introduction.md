# Machine Vision from the Global South: Synthetic Data and the Acquisition Limits of Armed-Person Detection in Dense Self-Built Settlements

## Abstract

Claims that aerial machine vision can tell an armed person from an unarmed one are acted on
without stating the acquisition conditions under which the distinction is visible at all. This
article treats that as the measurable question it is, using a procedurally generated synthetic
corpus of a dense self-built settlement.

Ground sampling distance, the ground area a single pixel covers, not scene realism, fixes the target. At the sensor and altitude of the
sensor and altitude specified in Section 7.3, with the camera pointing straight down at 150 m, a person occupies 17.6 by 17.5
pixels and a firearm is not resolvable within that region. Halving the altitude yields a 6.4-fold
increase in area, and the question becomes empirical rather than foreclosed.

The corpus is built to defeat the objection that separability in synthetic imagery is an artefact
of an invariant asset: five firearm models are set against nine elongated objects carried in the
same territory, brooms, rebar, pipe, umbrellas, drawn from the same colour and roughness palette,
so that no detector can separate the classes by colour. False positives on those hard negatives
are reported against altitude.

Transfer to real aerial imagery is reported as a ratio to what a detector trained on real data
achieves on the same test set, and that ratio is [X] per cent. No armed and unarmed accuracy is
reported at 150 m, because at that ground sampling distance the capability does not exist and a
figure obtained there would measure the regularity of our asset library. Stating the conditions
under which a capability is absent is a contribution of the same kind as demonstrating one that
is present.

**Keywords:** synthetic data, aerial object detection, ground sampling distance, dual use,
informal settlements

## Introduction

Aerial systems are already being bought and flown on the premise that a camera above a city can tell an armed person from an unarmed one. What is rarely stated alongside that premise is the altitude and the sensor at which the distinction is visible at all. Answering that requires a reading of policed urban space that the aerial literature has largely not offered, and the reason it has not is the subject of what follows.

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are deployed both for trajectory planning and navigation in complex environments, such as mountainous terrain, urban canyons, and no-fly zones, as well as for policing applications, in which UAVs are used for patrolling, crime deterrence, and target search, typically integrated with ground stations and patrol vehicles (Chen, Liu, Hu, Zhang, & Pan, 2025).

The recent literature on optimization- and computer-vision-based UAV trajectory planning reflects a growing concern with complex three-dimensional environments that cannot be reduced to flat terrain. Rasoulie and Pashazadeh (2026), for instance, propose a task-allocation and archive-guided mutation particle swarm optimization algorithm (TAMOPSO) for 3D UAV route planning, validated on synthetic terrains featuring multiple peaks, altitude variation, obstacles, and no-fly zones. Xu (2025) brings this discussion closer to the urban domain by comparing RRT, ACO, and A* algorithms, combined with Bézier-curve optimization, within a three-dimensional gridded urban environment explicitly designed for law-enforcement operations involving high-rise buildings and dynamic obstacles. Earlier, and in seminal fashion, Agrawal, Ratnoo, and Ghose (2017) had already shown that optical-flow-based navigation allows a forward-facing-camera UAV to react to walls and obstacles while traversing straight, L-shaped, and T-shaped urban canyons, through a steering command inversely proportional to the optical-flow difference between the two sides of the image. Complementing this line of work, the review by Jiang et al. (2026) on UAV GNSS positioning devotes a dedicated section to "Urban Canyons and Low-Altitude Electromagnetically Complex Environments," showing that tall buildings and narrow streets obstruct line-of-sight, generate severe multipath, and increase the incidence of NLOS signals, to the point that GNSS alone tends to be insufficient in these settings, reinforcing the need for fusion with inertial and visual sensors.

When the literature turns specifically to aerial policing, however, this three-dimensional complexity tends to dissolve into essentially horizontal formulations. Wang and Qian (2026) acknowledge, in their introduction, that police UAVs operate in "complex urban environments" and face considerable difficulty planning routes for multi-target search; yet the problem they actually model (UPPF) is a discrete variant of the traveling salesman problem, in which PSO particles merely optimize the visiting order of a set of target locations, with no explicit treatment of altitude, vertical obstacles, or urban-canyon geometry. This pattern recurs in Chen et al. (2025), whose model of UAV police patrolling, calibrated against six years of real crime data from Denver, treats the city essentially as a street network over which station allocation and patrol routes are optimized to maximize crime-deterrence effects; the core spatial unit of the problem is the street, not the three-dimensional airspace volume. Similarly, Yan, Hsieh, and Lai (2025) propose a routing and scheduling model that integrates UAVs and police vehicles through a time-space network technique, but again frame the problem around patrol points and routes and the maximization of crime-coverage rates, a fundamentally planar patrolling logic, inherited from ground-based policing, rather than a genuine engagement with the vertical complexity (peaks, urban canyons, maximum maneuvering angles) addressed in the more general trajectory-optimization literature reviewed above. The same planar inheritance reappears one level down, at the level of perception, in the assumption that an aerial view of a person is a view of a standing figure. Section 8.2 measures what nadir acquisition does to that assumption.

This persistent horizontality is not merely a modeling convenience; it reveals a structural blind spot in how the field conceptualizes policed urban space, one that becomes especially consequential when the terrain itself resists a two-dimensional reading. Urban informal settlements, commonly referred to in Brazil as *favelas*, represent one of the most persistent blind spots in official spatial data infrastructures. In cities such as Rio de Janeiro, *favelas* are frequently characterized by dense, vertically expanding, self-built housing that defies the conventional cartographic and remote-sensing methods designed for planned urban grids. This spatial informality has direct consequences for public administration, urban planning, and emergency response, and, more controversially, for public-security operations, which routinely take place in these territories with limited situational awareness of their built environment.

It is worth stressing that a vertically built *favela* is not simply a special case of mountainous terrain, and treating it as such risks eliding the specific computational and operational challenge it poses. A hillside covered in vegetation and a favela built on that same hillside do not constitute the same problem, either computationally or operationally. In a Rio de Janeiro favela, several sources of complexity co-occur: steep slopes; overlapping and vertically stacked construction; narrow alleyways; rooftop slabs (*lajes*) that are themselves informally occupied or extended; irregular stairways; buildings with no standardized geometry; access routes that are absent or poorly represented in official maps; obstacles both above and below the UAV's flight path; abrupt changes in local elevation over very short horizontal distances; visual occlusion between structures; degradation of GNSS/GPS signals; the operational need to maintain line of sight; moving pedestrians and other dynamic obstacles at street level; and, critically, an imperfect correspondence between what is observable from above and what is operationally relevant at the level of human circulation on the ground.

This distinction suggests a conceptual progression that the current literature has only partially traced: from (1) flat urban terrain, to (2) mountainous terrain, to (3) formal three-dimensional urban space, and finally to (4) vertically complex informal urban space. The fourth category remains markedly underdeveloped. UAV research on informal settlements does exist, but it is concentrated almost entirely in mapping, urban planning, and remote sensing, rather than in operational or security-oriented applications. Gevaert, Persello, Sliuzas, and Vosselman (2016), for example, integrated 2D radiometric, 2.5D topographic, and 3D geometric features extracted from UAV imagery specifically to overcome the classification difficulties introduced by dense buildings and steeply sloped terrain in an unplanned settlement in Kigali, Rwanda, an early acknowledgment that topography and informal vertical construction jointly, rather than separately, complicate machine-vision-based classification. More recently, Sithole and Mhangara (2025) used UAV imagery and object-based machine learning to classify the Slovo Park informal settlement in Johannesburg, South Africa, while a broader body of scholarship on digital mapping in informal areas, including systematic reviews such as da Silva, Kormann da Silva, and Westerholt (2024), has documented the expanding role of high-resolution aerial and drone-based data in this domain, albeit with an emphasis on participatory and planning outcomes rather than security applications. Taken together, this reveals two bodies of literature that rarely intersect. The first, UAV research in policing and defense contexts, which is organized around surveillance, reconnaissance, patrol, target tracking, situational awareness, and path planning, largely within formally mapped or geometrically regular urban space. The second, UAV research on informal settlements, that is organized around mapping, photogrammetry, remote sensing, three-dimensional reconstruction, urban planning, and building classification, largely divorced from operational security concerns. The intersection of these two literatures constitutes the gap this research addresses.

This article accordingly poses the following research question: **Under what acquisition conditions, and at what cost in transfer, does synthetic imagery of a dense self-built settlement support the aerial detection of an armed person?**

Our objective, correspondingly, is to establish the acquisition conditions (altitude, sensor, camera geometry, and illumination) under which a person observed from the air can be distinguished as armed or unarmed, and to measure what a detector trained on synthetic imagery alone recovers of that capability, using Jacarezinho, in Rio de Janeiro, as the morphology the synthetic corpus reproduces and as the site whose vertical complexity motivates the choice.

Two object classes are in play, and they should be separated at the outset. The vertical morphology of the settlement is what the model proposed in Section 6 is designed to classify, and it is the reason this site was chosen. The instrument evaluated in Sections 7 to 11 detects people, and at the lower of the two altitudes, armed people. Only the second has a public benchmark against which transfer can be measured, which is why it, and not the first, carries the empirical claims of this article.

This research is relevant to society and to urban studies because favelas remain systematically underrepresented in official geospatial datasets, which constrains equitable resource allocation for sanitation, risk mapping, and disaster response. It is also relevant to police institutions, because operations in vertically dense, informally built territories are widely documented as high-risk for all parties (officers, criminal actors, and, overwhelmingly, uninvolved residents), partly due to poor advance knowledge of the built environment. Finally, it is relevant to communities themselves, because the same technology that could, in principle, support safer emergency response or infrastructure planning also carries a serious risk of being repurposed for surveillance, profiling, or the operational targeting of residents who have not consented to being mapped. This dual-use tension is treated as a central, rather than peripheral, concern of this research.

The vantage point named in the title is not decorative. Three of the conditions this study works under would not arise in a study of the same problem in a mapped, consented and well-benchmarked city: there is no public benchmark for the morphological task at this scale (Section 11.4), the benchmark that does exist is an urban scene that is not a favela (Section 11.2), and no consented aerial imagery of the territory exists or can readily be obtained (Section 7.1). Working from the Global South here means working under those three absences, and much of what follows is an account of what they cost.
