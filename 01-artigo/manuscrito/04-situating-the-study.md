## Situating the Study: Violence, Policing, and Territorial Control in Rio de Janeiro



Rio de Janeiro presents a paradoxical security landscape. Homicide figures have fallen substantially over the past two decades, the state's intentional homicide rate dropped from a peak in the early 2000s to 17.1 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2024, a 10.8% decline from 2023 (Instituto de Segurança Pública [ISP-RJ], as cited in Vanguard Attaché, 2026), and homicides in the city itself fell from 2,574 in 2003 to 965 in 2025, a 63% reduction (Crime Brasil, 2026). This decline, however, coexists with a persistently high (and in 2025, rising) rate of lethal police intervention. The Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública's (2026) 20th Anuário Brasileiro de Segurança Pública reported 798 deaths from police intervention in Rio de Janeiro state in 2025, up from 703 the year before, and noted that violent deaths of police officers themselves rose 82.1% in the same period, the sharpest increase recorded nationally, with the state alone accounting for more than a third of all such deaths in Brazil (CNN Brasil, 2026a; Defesa em Foco, 2026).

By 2025, police-caused deaths accounted for 20.5% of all intentional violent deaths in the state, placing Rio among the small group of Brazilian states with the highest documented ratio of lethal force to overall violent mortality (Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública, 2026; Sousa, 2026). This dual trajectory (declining civilian homicide alongside sustained or increasing police lethality) is central to understanding why the technical and ethical questions this study raises cannot be separated from the operational environment in which any deployment would occur.

Large-scale police incursions into favelas controlled by criminal organizations have long been a recurring feature of public security policy in Rio de Janeiro, and their human cost has repeatedly drawn national and international scrutiny. On May 6, 2021, civil police carried out Operação Exceptis in the Jacarezinho favela, deploying roughly 200 to 250 officers, armored vehicles, helicopters, and a sniper in what was, at the time, the deadliest single police operation in the city's history: 27 civilians and one officer were killed (Human Rights Watch, 2021; Small Wars Journal, 2021). Independent witnesses and human-rights organizations, including Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, raised concerns that some of the dead had been executed rather than killed in exchanges of fire, and that all victims taken to hospital had already arrived dead (Al Jazeera, 2021; Human Rights Watch, 2021).

That record was surpassed four years later. On October 28, 2025, a joint civil and military police operation (Operação Contenção) was launched against the Comando Vermelho in the Complexo do Alemão and Complexo da Penha, mobilizing approximately 2,500 officers, armored and demolition vehicles, and helicopters to execute 51 arrest warrants across 26 communities (Agência Pública, 2025; Wikipedia, 2026). Official figures put the death toll at 121 to 122, including four to five police officers, making it the deadliest police operation in Brazilian history (CNN Brasil, 2026b; Global.org.br, 2025; Wikipedia, 2026). Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva described the outcome as "a massacre" and called for an independent investigation (CBS News, 2025), while the United Nations expressed "grave concern" over what it termed the deadliest police operation in the country's history (OPEB, 2025). Notably for the present study, the operation also marked a documented escalation in the technological means used by criminal organizations to counter police incursions: Comando Vermelho fighters used consumer drones to drop improvised explosives on police units during the confrontation, a tactic corroborated by multiple independent outlets and subsequently described by security analysts as an emerging regional pattern of drone use by organized crime in Latin America (CNN, 2026; Small Wars Journal, 2025). In direct response, Rio's Civil Police has since expanded its own Coordenadoria de Operações com Aeronaves Não Tripuladas (COANT), incorporating UAVs into intelligence gathering and real-time operational monitoring (CNN, 2026), a development that situates the present study's technical proposal within an active, rapidly evolving, and already contested operational reality rather than a purely hypothetical one.

These episodes are not aberrations but recurring features of a policy model that has been subject to sustained judicial intervention. In 2019, the Brazilian Socialist Party, together with civil society organizations, filed Arguição de Descumprimento de Preceito Fundamental (ADPF) 635 (popularly known as the "ADPF das Favelas") before the Supreme Federal Court (STF), seeking to curb the high number of civilian deaths resulting from police operations in Rio's favelas. In June 2020, Justice Edson Fachin, as rapporteur, issued a preliminary injunction restricting incursions during the COVID-19 pandemic and requiring that high-risk operations be conducted only in "absolutely exceptional" circumstances, with prior notice to the Public Prosecutor's Office (Agência Brasil, 2025; Movisafe América Latina, 2025). The case remained under the Court's supervision for nearly six years, during which the state was required to report disaggregated lethality data and to equip officers and vehicles with body cameras and geolocation systems. On April 3, 2025, the STF concluded its judgment, partially validating a state-presented plan to reduce police lethality while simultaneously reversing several of the operational restrictions imposed since 2020, mandating expanded Federal Police involvement in cases with interstate implications, and requiring the state to draft a plan for the territorial reoccupation of areas controlled by criminal organizations (Gazeta do Povo, 2025; STF, 2025a, 2025b). Assessments of the ruling's practical effect diverge sharply: legal analysts noted that lethality had already fallen by more than 60% between 2019 (1,814 deaths) and 2024 (699 deaths) under the Court's earlier restrictions (JOTA, 2025), a 2024 figure that differs from the 703 reported above by the Fórum Brasileiro de Segurança Pública (2026); the two series count differently, and the discrepancy is itself an instance of the difficulty official statistical infrastructures have in this domain, whereas critics on both ends of the political spectrum characterized the final decision as either insufficiently protective of favela residents or as an unwarranted judicial intrusion into operational policing (Causa Operária, 2025; Gazeta do Povo, 2025). Operação Contenção, occurring barely six months after this ruling, has since become a central data point in the ongoing debate over whether the ADPF 635 framework meaningfully constrains lethal police conduct in practice (Agência Pública, 2025).

Structurally, these outcomes reflect difficulties that predate any single operation or judicial ruling. Rio's security forces operate against criminal organizations, principally the Comando Vermelho, founded in 1979 within the Cândido Mendes prison and now estimated to have up to 30,000 members with territorial reach well beyond Rio de Janeiro (Wikipedia, 2025a), that maintain durable, often decades-long territorial control over specific favelas, financed largely through drug trafficking and increasingly contested by rival factions and expanding milícias (paramilitary groups frequently composed of active or former security personnel). This territorial permanence, combined with the built environment of the favelas themselves, has repeatedly been cited (by both security officials and independent researchers) as a central obstacle to policing: dense, self-built, vertically stacked construction obstructs sightlines and vehicle access, narrow alleys and improvised barricades slow or halt armored incursions, and the absence of reliable, up-to-date maps of informal circulation routes leaves operational planning dependent on outdated or incomplete intelligence (InSight Crime, as cited in Ad Infinitum, 2023; Small Wars Journal, 2021, 2025).
