## Literature Review

### Machine Vision and Informal Urban Environments

Machine vision applied to informal settlements has grown as a research niche at the intersection of remote sensing, computer vision, and urban studies. Several strands of recent literature ground this study.

In fact, a substantial body of work has used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and related architectures to detect informal settlements from very-high-resolution (VHR) and low-resolution multispectral imagery, motivated by the high cost of VHR data and the frequent absence of reliable ground-truth annotations. Gram-Hansen et al. (2019) proposed a semi-automated pipeline combining domain knowledge (e.g., known roofing materials) with machine learning classifiers to convert satellite imagery into binary informal-settlement maps, explicitly addressing the challenge of poor and inconsistent official records in developing-country contexts. More recent work has extended these pipelines toward multiscale and multimodal fusion: Du, Xing, Wang, Wei, and Zhang (2024) proposed a scene-classification-assisted, texture-feature-enhanced multiscale network for large-scale informal-settlement extraction from remote sensing imagery, while Hu, Huang, Ren, and Zhang (2025) went a step further by fusing satellite imagery with street-view data in a cross-scale hierarchical perception network, explicitly aiming to recover the fine-grained structural detail that overhead imagery alone cannot capture. A parallel line of work has turned to UAV-derived imagery specifically: Sithole and Mhangara (2025) used high-resolution drone imagery and object-based machine learning to classify the Slovo Park informal settlement in Johannesburg, South Africa, resolving structural patterns at a spatial resolution unattainable from satellite platforms — an approach directly relevant to resolving vertical structure, even though its object of classification remained the building footprint rather than its vertical extension.

The vertical dimension as an under-addressed problem. Much of the informal-settlement detection literature reviewed above treats settlements as two-dimensional footprints, optimized for horizontal delimitation rather than vertical stratification. This is a significant theoretical gap for contexts like Rio's favelas, where informal vertical growth (additional floors, rooftop units) is a defining and rapidly changing structural feature, with direct implications for population density estimation, structural risk, and tactical navigability — none of which is captured by planar classification alone. As early as 2016, Gevaert, Persello, Sliuzas, and Vosselman had already shown, in a study of an unplanned settlement in Kigali, Rwanda, that dense buildings and steeply sloped terrain jointly — rather than separately — challenge state-of-the-art classification algorithms, and that integrating 2D radiometric, 2.5D topographic, and 3D geometric features extracted from UAV imagery substantially improves classification accuracy in such conditions. Nearly a decade later, this concern with the vertical dimension has resurfaced in higher-impact venues focused on the Global South more broadly: Watson and Elliott (2025), writing in Scientific Reports, used high-resolution digital elevation models and deep-learning-based height prediction to reconstruct the vertical component of three cities — Nairobi, Kathmandu, and Quito — explicitly flagging the omission of Nairobi's Mukuru informal settlement from existing open building datasets, and arguing that constraining the building-scale vertical dimension of urban growth is essential for estimating population distributions and assessing exposure to natural hazards. Taken together, these two studies — nine years apart — suggest that the vertical dimension of informality has been intermittently acknowledged as important, but has not yet been systematically integrated into either the informal-settlement detection literature or the UAV trajectory-planning literature discussed earlier.

Synthetic data and the sim-to-real gap. Because labeled, consented, high-resolution imagery of vertical informal structures is scarce, this study draws on the domain randomization literature developed by Tobin, Fong, Ray, Schneider, Zaremba, and Abbeel (2017) and subsequently extended through structured domain randomization (Prakash et al., 2019), which uses real-world spatial priors to constrain the placement of synthetic elements and produce more contextually plausible — if not photorealistic — training scenes. More recent, domain-specific evidence indicates that this approach transfers well to aerial and UAV-centered perception tasks. Barisic, Petric, and Bogdan (2022), publishing in IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, generated a synthetic aerial dataset using texture randomization and reported consistent mean-average-precision gains for UAV object detectors evaluated on real imagery, particularly under difficult illumination and small-object conditions typical of aerial views. In a related vein, Horváth, Erdős, Istenes, Horváth, and Földi (2023), in IEEE Transactions on Robotics, demonstrated that a sim-to-real domain-randomization pipeline for object detection could shrink the reality gap to a satisfactory level, achieving mean-average-precision scores above 86% in zero-shot transfer and above 97% in one-shot transfer to real images. Together, this body of work confirms that domain-randomized synthetic training — especially when combined with a modest volume of real, fine-tuning data — can substantially close the sim-to-real performance gap for object detection tasks in complex, cluttered environments. This literature directly informs the synthetic-training strategy proposed in Section 7, since it offers a technically credible route to training a model without requiring extensive, consent-fraught real-world image collection inside an active informal settlement.

Positioning this study. Taken together, this literature suggests both a technical opportunity and an unresolved theoretical gap. On one hand, mature synthetic-training methods (Tobin et al., 2017; Prakash et al., 2019; Barisic et al., 2022; Horváth et al., 2023) could substantially reduce reliance on invasive, real-world data collection inside informal settlements. On the other hand, the vertical dimension of informality remains theoretically under-integrated (Gevaert et al., 2016; Watson & Elliott, 2025), and nearly all prior applications of machine vision to informal settlements have been framed around planning, humanitarian, or development purposes rather than security operations — a pattern also visible in the broader literature on digital mapping and community participation in informal areas (da Silva, Kormann da Silva, & Westerholt, 2024). This reveals two bodies of literature that rarely intersect: UAV research in policing and defense contexts, organized around surveillance, reconnaissance, patrol, target tracking, situational awareness, and path planning, largely within formally mapped or geometrically regular urban space; and UAV research on informal settlements, organized around mapping, photogrammetry, remote sensing, three-dimensional reconstruction, urban planning, and building classification, largely divorced from operational security concerns. This study's contribution, and its central point of critical reflection, is to examine what changes — technically, ethically, and socially — when a comparable machine vision approach is designed with an explicit public-security end use in mind, at the intersection of these two literatures.
