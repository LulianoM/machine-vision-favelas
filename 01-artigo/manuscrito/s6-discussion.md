## Discussion

### 6.1 Answering the research questions in light of the literature

**RQ1: transfer from synthetic imagery alone.** Without real training images, detection reached 1.6 per cent of the ceiling for the reference acquisition, 9.7 per cent with the same settlement seen obliquely, and 15.3 per cent over three runs of S-oblique-six, 16.7 for its best run (Section 5.2). Camera pitch moved the figure most: from S-nadir-150 to S-oblique by +0.0511 of mAP50, a factor of 6.0 (4.6 to 7.0) and 501.1 per cent, which passes the interval and relative readings of Section 4.7, the seed reading being inapplicable with one run per model. Six settlements instead of one moved it less, by +0.0088 at nadir (+0.0038 to +0.0102; ratio 1.866, 1.308 to 2.071; 86.6 per cent) and by +0.0353 obliquely on the seed mean (+0.0305 to +0.0411; ratio 1.575, 1.477 to 1.692; 57.5 per cent), the single-settlement value lying outside the range of the three runs. Both pass every reading available to them. Apparent size was matched (301 against 300 square pixels for the median instance), and matching the median long side as well moved the nadir figure by a quarter, below the half fixed in advance as the sign of a scale confound (Sections 5.1 and 5.2).

What remains once size is matched is shape. Seen from directly above, a person is the plan of a figure, as wide as it is long, with a median height-to-width ratio of 0.98 in both nadir corpora, against 1.95 and 2.20 for the standing figures of the benchmark (Section 5.1). We read the factor of 6.0 as the cost of that mismatch, with two reservations. The oblique arm is an adaptation of the corpus to the benchmark, calibrated on its training split, so it says what the benchmark rewards and not how the settlement should be flown. And the oblique camera changes altitude, the figure-placement filter and the GSD along with pitch (Section 4.6), so the factor belongs to the camera with the settings that depend on it.

Kiefer, Ott and Zell (2022) obtained 1.2 to 10.2 points of mAP50 on the same benchmark from game-engine images alone (Section 2.2), 23.2 per cent of real training for their YOLO row. Our oblique figures sit at the upper end of that range, 9.7 points for the mean of three runs of S-oblique-six, and just above it, 10.5 for the best run. The ratio to our own ceiling stays below theirs, on denominators that differ (1,106 images and one class against the full training split and ten classes). The direction agrees with the 30.4 points that removing variation in sun, weather and viewing angle cost Liao et al. (2026) on YOLOv11, without extending their claim, since one benchmark that is not the target domain cannot carry a general statement about camera position; their other ablation is taken up in Section 6.2.

**RQ2: the synthetic corpus once some real images exist.** With the 5 or 10 per cent slices of real images (Section 4.3), the synthetic corpus added no accuracy in either use (Section 5.3). As pretraining it finished below the generic start by 0.0716 of mAP50 on the seed means at 5 per cent, 15.8 per cent, and by 0.0614, 12.1 per cent, at 10; mixed in, by 0.0637, 13.8 per cent, and by 0.0281, 5.2 per cent. The expectation fixed before the run failed in sign: the difference is most negative at the smaller volume, where pretraining was expected to be worth most, and what narrows as real data grows is a cost. The pretraining difference passes the seed and interval readings of Section 4.7 at 5 per cent and the interval reading alone at 10, with one run per side. The joint cost passes the interval reading at both volumes, exceeds the margin fixed in advance and at 5 per cent lies outside the generic range. None of the four differences reaches the descriptive reading of 30 per cent (Section 5.6).

The sign agrees with the negative transfer reviewed in Section 2.2, and the joint models, which keep the generic initialisation, reduce the cost without removing it; whether the size, the variety or the image domain of the corpus decides the sign, or the one schedule that was run (Section 6.5), this design cannot say.

**RQ3: the armed and unarmed distinction by GSD.** On synthetic imagery alone, the detector separated figures carrying a firearm from figures carrying an ordinary elongated object only partially at 2.44 and 1.14 cm per pixel, and the question at 4.88 was not measured. S-nadir-75 called 0.237 of confusable objects armed on its selection split and S-nadir-35 called 0.273, against 0.165 for the twin of its corpus rendered at 75 m with every other setting held; on 80 new frames per corpus the matched pair gave 25 against 12.5 per cent (Section 5.4). Between one object in six and one in four was called armed on the selection splits, and finer resolution did not reduce the confusion. At 4.88 cm per pixel, where the median figure of the corpus of record measures 17.6 by 17.5 pixels, the one three-class model trained at that distance found 148 of 297 figures, below the validity condition of one half fixed before it was run, so its rates are descriptive and the absence at that distance rests on an argument from resolution.

The cue the detector uses is not established, and object length is the untested alternative to the form of a firearm: the five weapons are recorded at 0.20 to 1.05 m, the nine objects at 0.88 to 2.5 m, and objects longer than 1.15 m are re-angled in every pose (Section 4.2.2). Three observations point to length. The closed umbrella, at 0.88 m short enough to be carried in the weapons' poses, is the object most often called armed in the unseen-weapon test, 0.38 against 0.10 for brooms and 0.11 for pipe. The unseen rifle and submachine gun are called armed within the range of the seen long arms, while the pistol sits at 0.20. And the invalid model at 150 m, taken descriptively, called 0.34 of armed figures and 0.14 of objects armed, which at a distance where no firearm form survives would separate the classes by length.

Without a detector the classes did not separate (Section 5.1): the co-similarity matrices of a frozen generic extractor were flat at every GSD, and the AUC of armed against object was 0.525, 0.576 and 0.654 at 1.14, 2.44 and 4.88 cm per pixel, the last on the fewest crops. A nearest-centroid rule called 0.332 and 0.253 of objects armed under cross-validation at the two finer distances and, fitted on the training split, 0.331 and 0.231 on the selection splits against the detector's 0.273 and 0.165. Its d′, 0.23 to 0.46, stayed below the detector's 0.67 to 0.90 on the selection splits, both uncorrected. PCA to 2 or 10 dimensions and LDA did not exceed the detector, and the pooled vector of the trained backbone was as flat as the generic one. A similarity device therefore does not replace the detector. The one structure the generic space holds opposes figures carrying nothing to figures carrying anything, the same reading as the length cue above, and Section 6.6 takes up the detect-then-classify alternative.

Which differences are resolvable, by the criteria of Section 4.7, decides what the rates show. The object rate of S-nadir-35 against its twin passes all three readings on the new frames, +0.087 and 64.9 per cent on the seed means with non-overlapping ranges, and the interval and relative readings on the selection splits, where the ranges overlap. The rates for armed and unarmed persons are resolvable on neither set, the comparison with the original S-nadir-75 reverses between an IoU of 0.50 and 0.60, an artefact of annotation by the same criterion, and the difference between S-nadir-75 and the twin at 75 m is not supported (p = 0.19). None of the unresolved comparisons changes the answer: d′ against confusable objects lies between 0.77 and 0.97 across models and sets for the first run of each, with the log-linear correction, no threshold for calling the capability present was fixed in advance, and what the models share is the absence of a demonstrated capability, not a capability with errors of different size.

### 6.2 GSD and viewpoint against photorealism

The measurements of Section 5.1 reframe a design problem usually posed as one of scene realism, on the assumption that a corpus which looks more like a favela transfers better. GSD sets the size of the target (Table @tab{gsd}), and altitude is only one means of reaching a GSD. At its wide setting the reference camera gives 9.71 cm per pixel at 150 m; from the same height it reaches 4.88 at 2.0 times zoom, 2.44 at 4.0 times and 1.14 at 8.5 times of its 30 times optical range, its footprint shrinking to 22 by 12 m at the last, 73 times less area (Section 4.2.4, Table @tab{gsdzoom}). The scale-matched control of Section 5.2 shows that what still differs at a matched long side is not scale.

Viewpoint sets the shape. The ratio of 0.98 is stable across a factor of two in render resolution and of six in instance count, and it is a property of where the camera is placed rather than of how the scene is built: a generator could be made arbitrarily more photorealistic without moving it, and no appearance-based measure of realism detects it. The geometry check of Section 5.2 is the evidence that it matters: with everything but the camera and the settings that depend on it held, the ratio moved from 0.98 to 1.46 and mAP50 on the benchmark rose by a factor of 6.0. Neither variable is photorealism, and the second is the cheaper to manipulate; what both leave unmeasured is the cost of the generic geometry itself, which Liao et al. (2026) put at 27.0 points for their object class and which the protocol of Section 4.1 kept us from measuring for this one.

### 6.3 Implications for acquisition practice

Two measurements bear on illumination, both on synthetic imagery (Sections 5.1 and 5.5). Contrast against local clutter was highest under overcast and lowest under hard sun and rain, only that gap approaching one standard deviation between images, and on 120 new frames hard sun was the weakest regime for S-nadir-150, eight points of mAP50 below the next, and overcast the strongest, level with low sun. The condition fixed in advance for withdrawing the proposed mechanism, cast shadow from rooftop objects filling the background under a hard sun (Dare, 2005), was not met, but low sun, whose shadows are as sharp, scored level with overcast, and the hard-sun-only ablation was not run. No real aerial benchmark isolates the contrast: VisDrone has no illumination label (Zhu et al., 2022), DroneCrowd compares light only for counting (Wen et al., 2021), and Okutama-Action reports no results by condition (Barekatain et al., 2017). The evidence licenses a conditional: if the difficulty measured under hard sun holds on real imagery of a roofscape of this kind, a covered sky is preferable to a high sun for detecting people, provided that the cloud base leaves the flight in visual meteorological conditions.

Aerial survey specifications ask for cloud-free imagery and a solar elevation of at least 30 degrees (National Geodetic Survey, 2002) or 15 (RICS, 2021), selecting the regime found weakest here, hard sun at 46 to 74 degrees, and excluding one of the strongest, low sun at 9 to 25. Drone-mapping guidance prefers overcast light for three-dimensional reconstruction and full sun for orthomosaics, because it permits fast shutter speeds (Maes, 2025; Pix4D, n.d., 2022; Leitão et al., 2016; GeoCue, 2025), on a mixed empirical record (Dandois et al., 2015; Slade et al., 2025). The conditional extends the first preference to person detection at the cost the second names, a slower shutter or higher gain, not measured here.

Two caveats bound the conditional. The favourable regime is overcast, not rain, and in Brazil operation under precipitation is excluded in any case (Departamento de Controle do Espaço Aéreo [DECEA], 2026). Operations that answer to events rather than to a schedule, such as search and rescue (Broyles et al., 2022), cannot choose their light, so for them the finding bears on the robustness a detector needs rather than on when to fly. The implication concerns how a roofscape of this kind would be surveyed, on synthetic evidence and one detector; nothing in this article recommends acquisition over the territory (Section 6.4).

### 6.4 Dual use: the false-alarm rate as an estimate of harm

The object rate reported in Section 5.4 is the synthetic estimate, unmeasured on real imagery, of how often a system of this kind would mark a resident carrying a household object as armed, the error that Section 3.3 places at the ethical core. It is a lower bound (Section 4.7), and an operator would also see as alerts the armed boxes it leaves out. The threshold, 0.25, was not tuned, and the rates vary with the realisation of the settlement.

Two tests fixed in advance check whether the rate is an artefact of the generator (Section 5.4). Recall on firearm models the detector had never seen was 0.466 against 0.400 on the models it was trained on, a ratio of 1.16 against the withdrawal threshold of 0.80, with the pistol counted or set aside, so what it separated is not the asset library. Carrying every confusable object exactly as a weapon is carried left the object rate unchanged, 0.137 against 0.154 (exact McNemar test, p = 0.56). The generalisation that passes the first test, to length and silhouette rather than to the asset, is plausibly the one that produces the confusion.

The position this leaves is specific. Measured at 2.44 and 1.14 cm per pixel on synthetic imagery, the capability carries a false-alarm rate against ordinary objects that finer resolution did not reduce, and at 4.88 cm per pixel it has not been measured at all. The condition under which the 75 m figure itself would be withdrawn is stated above so that others can apply it.

### 6.5 Limitations

**1. The benchmark.** VisDrone records dense urban scenes from uncontrolled acquisition; the benchmark is not a favela, and a transfer figure on it says nothing about performance over Jacarezinho. It was captured at an estimated 30 to 100 m with the camera mostly tilted: in its training split only about 16 per cent of the annotated objects lie in bird-view images, against 84 per cent in images with an acute viewing angle (Kiefer, Messmer, & Zell, 2021; altitude range after Nguyen et al., 2026). The near-zero zero-shot scores of the nadir models measure a change of viewing geometry as much as a change of scene, and the nadir-to-oblique comparison sets two synthetic geometries against one real distribution without saying which transfers better in general. No public aerial benchmark with bounding boxes of people on land reproduces a nadir view at 150 m and about 5 cm per pixel. The closest are HERIDAL, nadir at 40 to 65 m and an estimated 1.5 to 2.4 cm per pixel (Božić-Štulić, Marušić, & Gotovac, 2019; Pyrro, Naseri, & Jung, 2021), and the Stanford Drone Dataset, nadir at about 80 m over a university campus (Robicquet, Sadeghian, Alahi, & Savarese, 2016). The per-frame altitude and gimbal pitch of SeaDronesSee allow nadir frames between 100 and 260 m to be selected, although its people are in the water (Varga, Kiefer, Messmer, & Zell, 2022).

**2. Synthetic realism.** One sensor was simulated, at two render resolutions, and the render at 4K is easier than a single frame from the camera it reproduces. The generator models geometry, materials, illumination and the optics of the camera; frames are stored losslessly as PNG and training tiles re-encoded once as JPEG at quality 92, and no sensor noise, motion blur, depth of field, rolling shutter or chromatic aberration is modelled (Section 4.2.4). The cost of the generic geometry is quoted rather than measured.

**3. Figure diversity and the invariant generator.** The human figures come from a pool of six rigged meshes per rendering run, recoloured, rescaled and posed per instance, and a carried item takes an orientation set in the frame of the body, so it does not always sit in the grip, and five weapon models stand against nine objects (Section 4.2.2).

**4. Runs and seeds.** Most models were trained once, five of them three times with training seeds on a fixed corpus, so the dispersion measured is that of training, and the settlement seed was never varied. Every benchmark figure carries a 95 per cent interval by resampling images (Section 5), but the nadir-to-oblique comparison still rests on one training run per side: its ratio has an interval of 4.6 to 7.0, and no dispersion between runs is known for either model.

**5. The fine-tuning configuration.** The fine-tuning and joint models share one untuned schedule at a fixed budget (Sections 4.5 and 4.6), applied alike to a start that is already a person detector and to one that is not, so the sign of Experiment B may be a property of the schedule.

**6. Selection against test.** The three-class models are scored on the split that selected their checkpoint (Section 4.5), except on the 80 new frames of the matched pair and the 50 of the unseen-weapon test. Since no benchmark of the kind Section 2.3 looked for exists, those scores are ceilings rather than evidence of transfer. The ceiling R itself selected its checkpoint on the benchmark split it is scored on, so every efficacy is a fraction of a slightly optimistic denominator, and the three-class rates are reported at an untuned confidence of 0.25 (Section 5.4 gives the full sweep).

**7. Controls not run.** The learning curve over corpus size was specified and not run, so whether 180 frames sit on the flat or the rising part of that curve is unknown, and the pilot on 40 scenes, which failed on its own domain, does not isolate between its two candidate causes, the small number of frames and two thirds of instances falling below one stride.

**8. Positionality.** One author's familiarity with Jacarezinho comes through a policing role (Section 4.1); triangulation against independent sources mitigates that bias without removing it, and no resident testimony was collected.

**9. One site, one city.** The study concerns one favela in one city, and nothing in it licenses an inference from Jacarezinho to informal urban verticality in general; the acquisition limits rest on GSD and on generic synthetic settlements rather than on properties of Jacarezinho, which is the sense in which the title speaks of dense favelas.

**10. Cues other than the weapon.** Every firearm shares the colour and roughness palette of the confusable objects, but the objects differ from the weapons in length and in how long items are angled (Section 4.2.2). A detector may therefore separate the classes by length or silhouette rather than by the form of a firearm; the unseen-weapon test and the crop-similarity analysis bear on this without excluding it (Sections 5.1 and 5.4).

**11. Sources.** The contextual material of Section 3 relies on journalistic and institutional reporting for recent events, and the population figures for Jacarezinho diverge by tens of thousands between sources, with no independent means of resolution here.

### 6.6 Future work

The design would be completed by a three-class measurement at 150 m on a corpus large enough to pass its validity condition, variation of the settlement seed, the learning curve and the hard-sun-only ablation. A fine-tuning schedule with a frozen extractor or a lower learning rate would test whether the cost of the synthetic start belongs to the schedule. A zero-shot evaluation of the nadir models on HERIDAL resampled to the target GSD, on the nadir frames of SeaDronesSee and on the Stanford Drone Dataset would separate the effect of viewing geometry from that of scene content. The aerial crowd images of DLR-ACD, nadir at 4.5 to 15 cm per pixel but annotated with points, allow only a counting-style check (Bahmanyar, Vig, & Reinartz, 2019). Validation against consented imagery of informal settlements elsewhere, of the kind already published for Kigali and Slovo Park (Gevaert et al., 2016; Sithole & Mhangara, 2025), would test generalisation to dense, sloped and irregular construction on a scene type closer to the target than the benchmark offers.

The armed class need not be decided by a three-class detection head. A two-stage alternative would detect the person first and then classify the crop by its similarity to class prototypes, so that the decision rests on a distance that can be inspected. Section 5.1 shows what that design cannot rest on: the pooled vector of a crop, generic or from the trained backbone, does not separate armed figures from figures carrying an object at any of the three GSDs. If the detector holds the information, it holds it locally, at the position and scale of the figure, so the second stage would need local features read from the detector's own map, which has not been tested.

Any imagery of the territory lies on the other side of a process that has not taken place: independent ethics review and community engagement outside the police institution (Section 4.1). What body would conduct it, what form consent would take in a territory under armed control, and whether meaningful consent is obtainable there are questions this study leaves open, and the next step is the design of that process, not a refinement of the detector.

The model of vertical morphology that motivated the choice of site, a classifier of building height, rooftop addition and material transition over a domain-randomised synthetic pipeline in the manner of Prakash et al. (2019), shares the generator, the annotation procedure, the randomisation and the scale analysis of this article. The generator already records, for every structure it builds, the footprint, the storey count and the roof height (Section 4.2.1). It was not built, and it has no evaluation set at this GSD, which is itself a finding; the two tasks share a pipeline and not an object class, so no figure reported here transfers to it.
