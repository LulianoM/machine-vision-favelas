# Machine Vision from the Global South: Synthetic Data and the Acquisition Limits of Armed-Person Detection in Dense Favelas

## Abstract

Public security operations in the favelas of Rio de Janeiro are among the most lethal in Brazil, for officers, for armed groups and, above all, for residents, and they take place in territory the police know poorly: dense, vertically built, self-constructed housing whose alleys, rooftops and occlusions official maps do not record. Aerial systems are already flown over these territories, and the decision to use lethal force there is taken here to turn on a visual judgement, whether a person is armed, that such systems are increasingly expected to support. Training a detector for that judgement is hard in this kind of urban zone: imagery of residents cannot be collected with meaningful consent, no public benchmark shows a favela or annotates a carried firearm at survey altitude, and the altitude, lens, camera angle and light of each flight decide what reaches the detector before any algorithm is chosen. Synthetic imagery avoids the consent problem, but what it delivers for public security, and under which acquisition conditions, had not been measured.

We treat that as a measurable question. On procedural corpora of a dense favela we characterised the target by ground sampling distance (GSD, ground width per pixel), camera pitch and illumination, trained detectors on synthetic imagery alone and scored them against a ceiling trained on as many real images, on an urban, mostly oblique benchmark and on a nadir one in wilderness; the confusion of firearms with ordinary elongated objects was measured synthetically. Straight down from 150 m (4.88 cm per pixel) a person spans 17.6 by 17.5 pixels and a firearm's form is argued to be lost. Synthetic-only training reached 2.2 per cent of the ceiling for that acquisition on the urban benchmark, 8.8 with the same settlement seen obliquely and 15.3 with six settlements (three-run means); on the nadir benchmark the order reversed, 62.7 per cent for the nadir corpus against 42.1 and 46.4 for the two oblique ones, with run ranges that overlap. With 5 or 10 per cent of real images, the synthetic corpus lowered accuracy, as pretraining under four fine-tuning schedules and mixed in. On held-out frames, at the default confidence and on the first training run of each model, detectors called 24.5, 12.5 and 25.3 per cent of ordinary objects armed at 4.88, 2.44 and 1.14 cm per pixel (150, 75 and 35 m), lowest at the middle resolution; at 150 m the weaker separation is read as a shortcut. Stating the conditions under which a capability has not been shown is a checkable contribution: the GSD and viewing geometry at which a claim was tested and the rate at which unarmed people are called armed are what an aerial claim of an armed person must declare before it enters a decision to use force, and what a police command, the court that supervises operations and the residents can require of it.

**Keywords:** public security, public administration, synthetic data, aerial object detection, ground sampling distance, dual use, informal settlements, favelas

## Introduction

### 1.1 Problem statement

Aerial systems are already flown over policed territory in Rio de Janeiro: the Civil Police flies unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) for intelligence and real-time monitoring of operations (CNN, 2026), where a single judgement, whether the person engaged was armed, is taken here as central to decisions to use lethal force (Section 3.5). It is increasingly proposed that a camera above a city can tell an armed person from an unarmed one, with drone datasets of armed persons built for law-enforcement use (Faheem et al., 2025; AlJundi et al., 2025) and game-engine corpora generated for aerial detection (Kim et al., 2026). What is rarely stated alongside that premise, and what decides whether the distinction is visible at all, is the acquisition condition; a claim that omits it cannot be checked. That condition has five terms: the altitude of the UAV; its sensor and lens; the ground sampling distance (GSD) that these produce, that is, the width of ground one pixel spans; the camera pitch, whether the camera points straight down (nadir) or obliquely; and the illumination. We treat it as a measurable question, on procedurally generated synthetic corpora of a dense favela.

Urban informal settlements, known in Brazil as favelas, are among the most persistent blind spots of official spatial data infrastructures: dense, vertically expanding, self-built housing defies the methods designed for planned urban grids (Gevaert, Persello, Sliuzas, & Vosselman, 2016; da Silva, Kormann da Silva, & Westerholt, 2024; Watson & Elliott, 2025). The same technology that could support safer emergency response there can be repurposed for surveillance, profiling or the targeting of residents who have not consented to being mapped (West & Bowman, 2016), a dual-use tension we treat as central. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 open that question: the first follows the technological turn in public security that brought aerial perception into policing, and the second states why this study trains on synthetic imagery rather than on imagery of residents.

The vantage point named in the title, the Global South, stands for three conditions of this study that would not arise in a mapped, consented and well-benchmarked city. No consented aerial imagery of the territory exists or can readily be obtained, so every training image is synthetic, a strategy whose transfer to real aerial imagery is known to be limited in dense urban scenes (Kiefer, Ott, & Zell, 2022) (Section 4.1). The public benchmarks on which transfer can be measured show either a dense urban scene that is not a favela, mostly from an oblique camera (Zhu et al., 2022), or people in wilderness seen from directly above (Božić-Štulić, Marušić, & Gotovac, 2019) (Section 6.5). Nor does any public test set exist for what distinguishes this territory, the vertical morphology of the settlement and what a person carries, at the GSD of a survey flight (Sections 2.3 and 6.6). Working from the Global South means working under those three absences.

### 1.2 Research gap

Closest to this problem, UAV research plans trajectories and patrols (Section 2.1). Route planning models three-dimensional obstacles (Rasoulie & Pashazadeh, 2026; Xu, 2025; Agrawal, Ratnoo, & Ghose, 2017), whereas aerial policing reduces the city to planar formulations whose spatial unit is the street, inherited from ground-based policing, not the airspace volume (D. Wang & Qian, 2026; Chen, Liu, Hu, Zhang, & Pan, 2025; Yan, Hsieh, & Lai, 2025). The same inheritance reappears at the level of perception, in the assumption that an aerial view of a person is a view of a standing figure (Section 5.1).

A vertically built favela is not a special case of mountainous terrain. A hillside covered in vegetation and a favela built on that same hillside do not constitute the same problem, either computationally (Gevaert et al., 2016) or operationally: stacked construction, narrow alleys and occlusion between structures co-occur there (Section 3.2), and what is observable from above corresponds imperfectly to what matters at street level. Of the progression from flat urban terrain, to mountainous terrain, to formal three-dimensional urban space, to vertically complex informal urban space, the fourth category remains underdeveloped.

UAV research on informal settlements exists, but for mapping, reconstruction and building classification rather than security (Gevaert et al., 2016; Sithole & Mhangara, 2025; da Silva et al., 2024; Watson & Elliott, 2025) (Section 2.1). The two literatures, one on surveillance and patrol over formally mapped space and one on mapping informal space, rarely meet, and their intersection is the gap this research addresses. On the administration side, the public administration literature has followed what an instrument does to the officer's discretion, with street-level bureaucrats trusting an algorithmic recommendation when it confirms their professional judgement (Selten, Robeer, & Grimmelikhuijsen, 2023) and with what a predictive system does shaped by the organisational practice around it (Meijer, Lorenz, & Wessels, 2021), on the premise that the instrument sees what it is said to see; over this terrain that premise is the open question (Section 3.3).

Section 2 reviews both literatures and the evidence on synthetic training for aerial detection, which transfers (Tobin et al., 2017; Barisic, Petric, & Bogdan, 2022) but, for small aerial targets, recovers only a fraction of what real data achieves (Kiefer, Ott, & Zell, 2022). What that evidence does not establish is twofold, and both parts are the empirical subject of this article: how much of the detection of a person a synthetic corpus of a dense settlement recovers on real aerial imagery, and by which parameters of acquisition and scene; and the scale at which what a person carries stops reaching a detector.

### 1.3 Objective and research questions

Our objective is twofold: to measure what a detector trained on synthetic imagery alone recovers of the detection of the person, on which any armed and unarmed distinction rests, and how the acquisition conditions (altitude, sensor and lens, the GSD they produce, camera pitch and illumination) move that fraction; and to establish, on synthetic imagery and from a nadir camera, at which GSD a figure carrying a firearm can be told from one carrying an ordinary object. Jacarezinho, in Rio de Janeiro, is the site whose vertical complexity motivates the choice and whose general morphology, though not its geometry, the synthetic settlements approximate (Section 3.2).

Three research questions follow; Section 4.6 defines the experiments, A to D, that answer them. The first two are posed against the transfer evidence of Section 2.2: training on synthetic imagery alone recovers a fraction of real training on a dense urban benchmark (Kiefer, Ott, & Zell, 2022), the fraction depending on the geometry and the rendering variation of the corpus (Liao et al., 2026), and once a few real images exist a synthetic corpus has given its largest gain (Kiefer, Ott, & Zell, 2022) or lowered accuracy below real-only training (Shen et al., 2023a, 2023b).

**RQ1.** How much of the person-detection accuracy obtained with real training images does a detector trained only on synthetic imagery of a dense settlement recover on public real aerial benchmarks, and which parameters of acquisition and scene (camera pitch, apparent target size, settlement variety) determine that fraction? Experiment A answers it; Experiment D adds, on synthetic frames only, how detection varies with illumination.

**RQ2.** Once a small number of real training images is available, 5 or 10 per cent of the benchmark's training split, does the synthetic corpus add accuracy, used as pretraining or mixed into the training set? Experiment B answers it.

**RQ3.** On synthetic imagery alone, at which GSDs (4.88, 2.44 and 1.14 cm per pixel from a nadir camera, the GSDs that this article's altitude labels of 150, 75 and 35 m denote, Section 4.2.4) can a detector separate a person carrying a firearm from one carrying an ordinary elongated object, and at what rate does it call the latter armed? Experiment C answers it.

The armed and unarmed distinction was studied on synthetic imagery only, because no public aerial benchmark annotates a carried firearm at these GSDs, the aerial datasets that label an armed person being recorded within a few metres of the ground (T. Li et al., 2021; Torkst et al., 2026) and the drone datasets with annotated firearms restricted or unreleased (Faheem et al., 2025; AlJundi et al., 2025), and the transfer measured under RQ1 and RQ2 is that of person detection. The classification of the settlement's built form, which the site invites, has no evaluation set; Section 6.6 states it as future work.

### 1.4 Contributions and organisation of the paper

The article makes five contributions. First, a characterisation of the aerial target by GSD, camera pitch and illumination that depends on no detector. Second, transfer fractions for person detection against a ceiling trained on a matched number of real images, rather than against the full real training split of Kiefer, Ott, and Zell (2022), nadir against oblique and one settlement against six, on an urban benchmark seen mostly obliquely and on a nadir one, where the order of the two geometries reverses, with run ranges that overlap. Third, the finding that on the urban benchmark the synthetic corpus lowered accuracy once 5 or 10 per cent of real images existed, as pretraining under four fine-tuning schedules and mixed in, a negative transfer (Z. Wang et al., 2019) of the kind recorded for mixing in low-shot aerial training (Shen et al., 2023a, 2023b). Fourth, a synthetic measurement of the confusion between firearms and ordinary elongated objects, a design with precedents at ground level or a few metres above it (Pérez-Hernández et al., 2020; Torkst et al., 2026), at the GSDs of 2.44 and 1.14 cm per pixel (75 and 35 m), lowest at 2.44, replicated on held-out frames and, at those two GSDs, across training runs, and at 4.88 cm per pixel (150 m) a weaker separation which, since a firearm's form is argued to be lost there, is read as a shortcut rather than as a capability. Fifth, the position that a statement of the conditions under which a capability has not been shown is a checkable contribution, of the same kind as demonstrating one, and the one that bears on dual use: it answers the premise left open in Section 1.2, that the instrument sees what it is said to see, by stating what a police command, the court that supervises police operations in Rio de Janeiro (Section 3.1) and the residents can require of an aerial claim of an armed person before it enters a decision to use force (Sections 6.4 and 7).

Section 2 reviews the related work, Section 3 presents the study context, Section 4 describes the materials and methods, including the four experiments and the criteria fixed before each was run, and Section 5 reports the target characterisation and then one experiment per subsection. Section 6 discusses the results against the literature and states the implications, the limitations and the future work in one place, and Section 7 answers the three questions.
